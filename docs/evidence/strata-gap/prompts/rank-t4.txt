Follow-up on the GPS explanation. Now compare the four global navigation satellite systems and dig into the practical accuracy question.

Part A - constellations:

Build a comparison of GPS (USA), GLONASS (Russia), Galileo (EU), and BeiDou (China):
- Number of satellites and orbital planes for each; which are still being deployed or modernized and as of what approximate vintage.
- Orbital altitude and inclination differences, and what higher or lower inclination means for visibility at 60 degrees north versus near the equator.
- Signal frequencies and band naming (L1/L2/L5, G1/G2/G3, E1/E5/E6, B1/B2/B3), which bands are shared or interoperable between systems, and why a modern receiver can combine all four constellations in one fix.
- Civil versus restricted service status for each, including whether encryption or degradation has ever been applied (mention SA for GPS and the GLONASS civilian signal history).
- Typical stated open-service accuracy for each system individually, and what a multi-GNSS receiver achieves by using them together - explain the mechanism: more satellites improves geometry (lower DOP), it does not by itself shrink the per-range error.

Part B - error budget arithmetic:

Take your earlier table of error sources for a consumer receiver and work the numbers through for two scenarios:
1. Standalone GPS, single frequency: start from per-component values (satellite clock+ephemeris, ionosphere with broadcast model, troposphere, multipath, receiver noise), root-sum-square them into a user equivalent range error, then multiply by a mid-range GDOP of, say, 1.4 for horizontal dilution to get a horizontal error estimate. Show every step of the arithmetic and state assumptions.
2. Dual-frequency receiver with ionosphere-free combination: recompute the UERE and the resulting position error, and quantify how much of the total the ionosphere was in scenario 1.

Then invert it: if you need 1 m horizontal 95% confidence, what ranging error and what GDOP do you need, and which single correction (dual-frequency, RTK, or SBAS) buys you the most improvement per unit of cost or complexity?

Part C - augmentation precision ladder:

Explain the ladder from plain SPS to SBAS to DGPS to PPP to RTK to PPP-RTK. For each rung: what data is transmitted (pseudorange corrections, carrier-phase corrections, atmospheric grid, orbit/clock from a global network), what its convergence time or initialization time is, what accuracy it reaches (horizontal, 95%), and what its infrastructure dependency is (geostationary satellite, internet, local base station). Part D - failure modes:

Describe three real-world cases where GNSS gives a confident but wrong answer: urban multipath creating a stable biased fix; spoofing (meaconing vs. overt carrier spoofing) and how civil receivers are vulnerable; and jamming with weak-signal amplification (meaconing through a repeater). For each: the signature a monitoring system would look for, and one mitigation that exists today (multi-antenna direction finding, INS coupling, authentication like Galileo OSNMA or GPS Chimera). Note explicitly which of these mitigations are live versus experimental.

Close with your bottom line: for a phone, for a survey rod, and for an autonomous car, which rung of the ladder each device actually sits on and why the price gap between rungs is so steep.
