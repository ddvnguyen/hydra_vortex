New topic again - this time biology and energy systems. Explain how a modern heat pump heats a house, why it can be more than 100% efficient without violating thermodynamics, and where it loses.

Structure your answer as follows:

1. The refrigeration cycle, in plain terms. Walk through the four components - compressor, condenser, expansion valve, evaporator - and for each one state: what enters (phase, pressure, temperature), what leaves, and where heat is absorbed or rejected relative to the room or the outside air. Use a concrete example with round numbers: outdoor 0 C, indoor 20 C, a house needing 5 kW, and a refrigerant like R32 or R290 with its approximate operating pressures. Show that the compressor work is roughly 1.5-2 kW in that example and explain where the other 3.5 kW came from (extracted from outside air, including the latent heat in moisture that condenses or frost that forms on the outdoor coil).

2. The efficiency metrics and the thermodynamics. Define COP and SCOP, and the seasonal difference (rated at a single point versus averaged over a winter's worth of bin temperatures). Then explain the Carnot/Carnot-like limit: a Carnot COP between a hot reservoir at 320 K and a cold one at 273 K is about 7.1, real machines achieve 3-5 in heating mode, and state which irreversibilities eat the gap (compressor isentropic efficiency, pressure drops, heat-transfer delta-T at both exchangers, valve throttling). The "more than 100%" claim: state clearly that the 300% figure is performance ratio of heat delivered to work consumed, that the extra energy is environmental heat, and that the first and second laws are respected - no perpetual motion. Mention that the same machine in reverse (air conditioning) is judged by EER/SEER and that the units differ.

3. Cold-climate behavior. Why capacity falls and COP falls as outdoor temperature drops (smaller temperature lift means better COP, but lower suction pressure means less mass flow and less density - two opposing effects). What inverter-driven variable-speed compressors change (modulate to match load, longer runtime, fewer cycles, better latent control). What happens at the balance point where the heat pump no longer covers the load, and the role of auxiliary or backup resistance heat - and why oversizing the backup is expensive to run. Explain defrost cycles: why frost forms on the outdoor coil below about 5 C with humidity present, how reverse-cycle defrost or hot-gas defrost works, the capacity interruption it causes, and the control logic (timer versus delta-T versus predictive).

4. System types. Compare air-source, ground-source (horizontal loop, vertical bore), and water-source heat pumps: typical installed cost order of magnitude, COP range for each, land or drilling requirements, and why ground-source wins on COP but often loses on payback for a single family home. Mention the refrigerant question: GWP of R410A versus R32 versus R290, the F-gas phase-down direction in regulation, and the flammability tradeoff of hydrocarbons.

5. Integration and numbers. A house with a heat loss of 6 kW at design temperature: size the heat pump, decide the thermostat setback strategy (why setback hurts a heat pump - recovery load plus condensate on cold walls), describe weather compensation (flow temperature curve) and why lower flow temperatures (35 C floor loops versus 55 C radiators) materially raise seasonal COP. Give a rough seasonal cost comparison against a gas boiler at 90% efficiency with gas at a stated price per kWh versus electricity at a stated price - show the break-even electricity price where the heat pump matches the boiler, and note which way the ratio moves if the boiler is old (70%) or the electricity carries a higher standing charge.

Finish with a short FAQ of three common misconceptions (heat pumps do not work in the cold; they blow lukewarm air so they must be weaker; turning them down when away always saves money) and a one-paragraph correction for each. Use numbers everywhere, keep equations simple, and label any figure you are unsure of as approximate.

Add a fourth FAQ entry with a worked number: a homeowner asks whether adding 100 mm of loft insulation is better value than replacing their boiler with a heat pump - give the rough heat-loss reduction from the insulation (state the starting U-value and the room count you assume), the annual kWh each measure saves at a stated efficiency and tariff, and your honest recommendation about doing both in which order, including the point that a well-insulated house lets you install a smaller, cheaper heat pump. Keep the arithmetic visible so they can redo it with their own bills.
